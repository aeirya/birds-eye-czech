THE GOOD NEWS

Czech spelling is mostly honest.

Once you relearn about a dozen letter values, Czech is dramatically more predictable than English. The difficult part is not random spelling: it is a handful of systematic spelling rules, especially ě, softening after i/í, vowel length, and voicing changes.

SIX RULES BEFORE THE ALPHABET

The mental model

1

Stress ≠ vowel length

Stress is normally on the first syllable. The marks in á é í ó ú ů ý mean the vowel is longer, not stressed.

2

Do not reduce unstressed vowels

Unlike English, an unstressed Czech vowel normally stays a clear vowel. Do not turn everything into uh.

3

i and y sound alike

Standard Czech normally pronounces i = y and í = ý. Their spelling distinction is mostly historical and grammatical.

4

ě modifies what comes before

Do not invent a new “ě vowel”. It usually signals palatalisation or a hidden j-like sound.

5

Spelling can stay voiced while speech devoices

At the end of a word, a written voiced consonant is typically pronounced voiceless: led → “let”, muž → “muš”.

6

r and l can be the vowel

Czech can build a syllable around r or l. So krk, vlk, and prst are pronounceable without inserting an extra vowel.

VOWELS

Simple qualities; important length

Czech vowels are much closer to the clean vowel systems of Spanish, Basque, Persian or Japanese than to English. The new thing is that length itself can distinguish words.

THE LETTERS MOST LIKELY TO TRICK YOU

Decode these first

c

/ts/

Like the final sound of English cats, but it can start a word.

Spanish warning: Czech c is never Spanish c /k, θ, s/.

č

/tʃ/

English/Spanish ch; Basque tx; Persian چ.

š

/ʃ/

English sh; Basque x; Persian ش.

ž

/ʒ/

English vision; Persian ژ. Much easier if Persian is in your sound inventory.

j

/j/

English y in yes; Persian consonantal ی; Japanese y.

Never English j /dʒ/.

ch

/x/

Spanish j is an excellent approximation; Persian خ is even more direct.

Never English “ch”. Czech “ch” is č.

h

/ɦ/

Usually voiced, unlike ordinary English /h/. Keep the throat open and add voice.

Important contrast: h ≠ ch.

r

/r/

A tongue-tip tap/trill. Spanish r/rr gives you the right articulatory family.

Do not use the English approximant r.

ř

/r̝/ — the Czech special

A raised, fricated trill: roughly a tongue-tip r happening together with a ž-like friction. It is not simply “r + zh” in sequence.

For early survival Czech, an ordinary rolled r is a more useful fallback than freezing every time you see ř. Improve it later.

ň

/ɲ/

Very close to Spanish ñ. The tongue body contacts the hard palate.

ť

/c/

A palatalised voiceless stop. Think of a very soft t made with more of the tongue against the hard palate.

ď

/ɟ/

The voiced partner of ť: a very soft/palatal d. Do not pronounce it as English “dj”.

WHEN THE LETTERS ARE NOT THE WHOLE STORY

Predictable hidden pronunciation rules

di / ti / ni are soft

In native Czech words, written d, t, n + i/í normally represents the palatal sounds ď, ť, ň.

ti→ťi

·

dě / tě / ně are also soft

Here ě signals the same palatalisation: read them approximately as ďe / ťe / ňe.

dě→ďe

·

bě / pě / vě / fě hide a j

After these labials, ě is normally heard approximately as je: bje, pje, vje, fje.

bě→bje

·

mě is special

mě is pronounced /mɲɛ/ — many learners hear something roughly like mňe.

mě→mňe

·

Final devoicing

At a pause, voiced obstruents lose their voicing. The spelling stays morphological.

b → pd → tď → ťg → kz → sž → šh → ch

·

Consonants copy voicing in clusters

Neighbouring obstruents tend to agree in voicing, usually according to the following consonant. So spelling can be more stable than the surface sound.

For now: imitate whole words. You do not need to calculate every assimilation while speaking.

USE SOUNDS YOU ALREADY OWN

Czech through languages you know

🇬🇧 English

Useful: č = ch, š = sh, ž = sound in vision.

Unlearn: j is not English j; c is not k/s; ch is not “ch”; r is not English r.

Biggest rhythm change: stop reducing unstressed vowels to schwa.

🇪🇸 Spanish

Useful: clean vowels, r/rr articulation, ñ ≈ ň, Spanish j ≈ Czech ch, Spanish ch = Czech č.

Unlearn: Czech c is always /ts/; Czech j is /j/ like English y, not Spanish jota.

New: phonemic vowel length and much heavier consonant clusters.

🟩 Basque

Very useful: tx ≈ č, x ≈ š, and Basque affricate spellings make Czech c /ts/ conceptually less strange.

Shared advantage: clear vowels and comparatively transparent spelling.

Careful with j: Basque j has strong dialect variation, so it is a bad universal anchor for Czech j.

🇮🇷 Persian

Excellent anchors: چ ≈ č, ش ≈ š, ژ ≈ ž, خ ≈ ch, consonantal ی ≈ Czech j.

Useful: a tongue-tip r is already much closer to Czech r than English r.

New: Czech writes essentially every vowel and marks long vowels explicitly.

🇯🇵 Japanese

Useful: phonemic vowel length is already a familiar idea; Japanese y gives a good model for Czech j.

Helpful sound: Japanese つ demonstrates that /ts/ can behave as a single consonantal unit; Czech c is /ts/ without the following u.

Opposite challenge: Czech tolerates enormous consonant clusters and syllabic r/l.
